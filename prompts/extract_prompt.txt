THE READER AND WHAT TO EXTRACT
Whoever reads these principles regards the speaker as his most trusted guide. Wring the speaker's words dry: extract every principle, idea, directive, piece of advice and warning he gives, in any domain and in whatever form he states it in words, and let nothing fall that could matter to someone who sees him that way. A principle here is any passage in which the speaker states an idea, a directive, a piece of advice, a warning, or an explanation he gives in order to teach. Read as that reader would, attentive to what the speaker means and to the context in which he says it. Do not decide what is "important", and do not leave something out because it is short, said in passing, said before elsewhere, or said inside a story. You never compose a principle: a lesson that a story only implies, and that the speaker never says in words, cannot be extracted.

HOW TO WRITE EACH PRINCIPLE
1. Verbatim. A principle is one continuous passage copied exactly from the transcript: same words, same order, same spelling. Never change, correct, skip words inside, reorder, add to, or join two separate places. Choosing where the passage starts and ends is not a change; the only change allowed to a word is the one in rule 5. Inside the passage, leave out only a stutter fragment (a lone letter left hanging right before a word that starts with the same letter, or the bare article "ال" left hanging before the full word); everything else inside, repeated words, fillers and quotation marks included, is copied exactly as written, not tidied or smoothed. Everything inside the passage stays, including an aside that interrupts the thought. Only what comes before the thought begins, or after it ends, is left out: end the passage before it or start it after it.
2. Complete thought. It holds the whole thought: the condition, the directive, the limit, and the reason the speaker gives for it. Do not end on an unfinished sentence, on filler, on a false start, or on an aside that is not part of the idea. When the speaker closes the idea by repeating its last phrase or sentence for emphasis, that repetition belongs to it. Decide from the meaning of the words, never from punctuation alone.
3. Start and end. Start at the first word the principle needs and end at the last word it needs. One test decides in both directions: would someone who never heard the rest of the talk lose meaning if this part were missing? If yes, keep it, and widen to include it; if no, leave it out. So leave out introductions, history, applause lines, the conclusion of a case or an example that the statement then explains, the words that only tie it to what came before (حتى، لأن، يعني، وبالتالي), and a separate example or story that follows. Keep what changes what the statement says or whose it is: the phrase that names the source or authority of a saying, the subject it speaks about, and a short example or number that sits inside the rule or in the same sentence. The emphasis repetition described in rule 2 is the one exception to that test: it stays.
4. Stands alone. It must make sense to someone who never heard what came before. Do not open on a word that points back ("this", "that", "so", "he", "she"); start at the statement itself, or earlier, so that the passage includes what the word refers to. A passage that points at something the speaker is showing or has just shown (a drawing, a board, a screen) is fine: if a screenshot of it was taken, it is delivered with the principle.
5. Attached connective letter. If a passage opens with a connective letter attached to its first word ("ف" or "و", as in فلو، فأنت، ولو، وأنت) and the thought is whole without it, you may start at the next letter. That one letter is the only thing that may be dropped from a word.
6. One idea per principle. If a passage contains two separate ideas, write two principles.
7. Restatements. If the speaker says a sentence and, right after it in the same passage, takes it back or restates it more completely or more precisely, extract the later formulation only. If the same idea returns in another place, even in nearly the same words, extract it there too. If you cannot tell whether the second sentence repeats the first for emphasis (rule 2) or restates it (this rule), keep both in one passage.
8. No overlap. No two principles share words of the transcript. If two passages cover the same stretch of speech, give the single clearer and more complete one. If a smaller idea sits inside a larger one, either it is part of the larger idea, and the larger principle covers it, or it is a separate idea, and rule 6 applies: split them so that they do not overlap. If the smaller idea sits in the middle of the larger one and cannot be split off without cutting the larger passage in two (rule 1 forbids joining two places), the larger principle covers both and you write no separate principle for the smaller one.
9. Unclear speech. A principle never contains the marker [غير واضح]. If the marker falls inside a passage, take the part on either side of it that holds a whole thought by itself (rules 2 and 4), each as its own principle, and leave the rest out.
